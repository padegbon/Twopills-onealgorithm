Red Pill, Pink Pill

Who profits when gender becomes a war between men and women?

By Oluwapamilerin Adegbonmire

Start reading ↓
REDPINK
01 / Start here

What if the gender war is also a business model?

A scroll through social media can make it seem as if men and women are natural enemies. One video says feminism has ruined relationships. The next says women must never trust men. Another promises to teach men how to become “high value,” or women how to win at a dating market. Each post offers a simple explanation for complicated experiences.

There are real issues underneath these conversations: loneliness, rejection, economic insecurity, unequal care work, gender-based violence, and uncertainty about changing expectations. People deserve spaces to discuss those experiences. The problem begins when personal frustration becomes a sweeping story about an entire gender, and a creator sells an audience a formula for winning the conflict.

When every problem is framed as proof that the other gender is the enemy, understanding becomes harder and outrage becomes easier to sell.

This post looks at two online currents often described as the manosphere and the femosphere. Neither is a single movement, and their communities vary widely. The focus here is on the strands that reduce gender relations to rigid hierarchies, resentment and competition, and on what they mean for gender justice around the world.

02 / The red pill

“Waking up” to a story about power

The phrase comes from the 1999 film The Matrix, where taking the red pill means seeing reality as it is. The film’s creators, Lilly and Lana Wachowski, are trans women, and Lilly has said the story was about the trans experience. Online, “red pill” now describes a supposed awakening to the “truth” about gender and dating: that men are disadvantaged, that feminism is to blame, and that women are motivated by status, money or control.

The manosphere is an umbrella term, not one organization. It includes men’s rights communities, pick-up artist forums, incel spaces, men going their own way, dating coaches, fitness and self-improvement creators, and more openly male-supremacist groups. Researchers Michael Vallerga and Eileen Zurbriggen read about 400,000 words from two of these forums and found a shared belief that men and women are different by nature, with men logical and women emotional, selfish and manipulative. The authors say the advice is presented as secret knowledge but is really an old stereotype taken further, and they found costs for men too, including talk of depression and suicide on the incel forum.

These spaces do not all hold identical beliefs. Research commissioned by Ofcom, the UK’s communications regulator, found a fragmented landscape where humour, debate and self-improvement content draw men in. (It involved only 38 men and one woman, and critics note that more misogynistic men may have declined to take part.) The underlying need is real: Equimundo’s 2023 survey found that two-thirds of young men feel that no one really knows them.

Support without scapegoating

Men discussing loneliness, friendship, emotional health, fatherhood, or pressure around work and identity can create useful support. These conversations do not require blaming women or dismissing feminism.

Hierarchy disguised as truth

Some content treats women as a category to manage or rank, presents dominance as the measure of manhood, and describes equality as a loss for men. Personal disappointment becomes evidence of a universal gender conspiracy.

Criticizing misogyny should not mean dismissing men’s problems. It means asking whether a proposed solution actually helps people, or just redirects their frustration toward a convenient target.

03 / The pink pill

When empowerment becomes another rigid script

“Pink pill” is used in different ways online, so it does not describe one consistent movement. It can refer to women-centred dating advice, anti-male or separatist spaces, and content that presents heterosexual relationships as a strategic contest. Researcher Jilly Kay calls the wider network the “femosphere.” It includes r/RedPillWomen, the Female Dating Strategy forum (about 260,000 members, which began as a reaction to The Red Pill) and influencers who promote “feminine energy” and traditional roles. A 2025 research review also notes a growing presence of anti-feminist “trad” women’s accounts.

Some women turn to these spaces after sexism, harassment, unequal relationships or disappointing partners, and Kay notes that some use the advice as protection from the manosphere. Advice about boundaries, financial independence, safety and self-respect can be valuable. Yet some creators replace one rigid script with another: men are portrayed as inherently untrustworthy, relationships become transactions, and women are encouraged to measure their worth through status, appearance or the ability to secure a wealthy partner. A 2026 preprint on r/RedPillWomen (not yet peer reviewed) found members using evolutionary psychology to defend traditional roles and raised concerns about women’s autonomy.

Not a false equivalence. Misogyny and anti-male generalizations do not operate in identical social conditions or carry the same histories and institutional power. Recognizing harmful rhetoric in women-centred spaces does not erase structural gender inequality or make the two phenomena interchangeable.

The useful question is not whether women should be allowed to criticize men. Of course they should. It is whether advice expands a person’s choices and agency, or narrows their world into suspicion, competition and fixed ideas about what men and women are “naturally” like.

04 / Research in focus

Case study: how boys meet the manosphere

Milne & Baker · 2025

From “villains” to “idols”: exploring teenage boys’ conflicting attachments to manospheric masculinities

Researchers Betsy Milne and Catherine Baker ran focus groups and follow-up interviews with young people aged 12 to 17 in four London schools, asking how they responded to a well-known manosphere influencer and to wider ideas of exaggerated masculinity. The article appeared in the journal Gender and Education.

What makes the findings important?

Condemning one influencer is not the same as rejecting the ideas. Many boys “othered” the influencer and condemned his misogyny, yet several still showed support, on a continuum, for content promoting sexist gender roles and exaggerated masculine ideals.

Humour does work. The researchers describe how joking made gendered power hierarchies feel normal and less serious.

So the response has to go further. The authors argue that interventions should go beyond condemning single influencers and look at the wider, increasingly networked subordination of women, femininity and Queer identities online.

Limitation. This is a qualitative study of a small group of young people in four schools in one city. It gives detailed insight into how some boys interpret this content. It cannot tell us how common these views are, how all boys think, or what happens in other countries and on other platforms.
05 / Follow the money

Anger has an audience. An audience has a price.

The online gender war is not only an argument about beliefs. It can also be an attention economy. Creators compete for clicks, shares, subscriptions and loyalty. A careful discussion of structural inequality takes time. A clip promising to expose “what women really want” or “why men are being replaced” is a faster emotional hook.

A 2025 review by Vivian Gerrand, Debbie Ging, Joshua Roose and Michael Flood describes four developments that separate today’s “neo-manosphere” from the earlier one: moves to new platforms, mainstreaming and monetization, ideological and ethnic diversification, and overlap with other extreme ideologies, most of them driven by recommender algorithms. In their summary of the review, the authors describe the pitch as two steps: direct your anger at women and feminism, then buy the influencer’s products. Those products can include dating coaching, paid communities, supplements, books and merchandise.

Experiments suggest how quickly the algorithms can deliver the audience. Researchers at Dublin City University made new TikTok and YouTube Shorts accounts posing as teenage boys and young men.

Both studies are small, with 10 accounts and 29 participants, and TikTok says the Dublin results do not reflect typical use. They show a risk, not an exact rate. A simple way to picture the business model:

1. Name a crisis

Tell viewers that dating, masculinity or society is broken, and that they are losing a competition they did not know they were in.

2. Offer a simple explanation

Blame feminism, women, “weak men” or a corrupt social order instead of looking at several interacting causes.

3. Sell the solution

Offer exclusive advice, paid access, courses, coaching or a community that promises transformation.

4. Keep the audience invested

Use new controversies and perceived threats to encourage repeat viewing, sharing and purchases.

This does not mean every creator who earns money from relationship advice is exploiting an audience, or that every platform deliberately promotes misogyny. The four steps are a simple model, not a finding of the review. The careful point is that attention-based business models can reward emotionally charged content, and some creators have learned to turn gender anxiety into a product.

06 / A global lens

There is no single global experience of gender

Online gender politics crosses borders, but it does not land the same way everywhere. Ideas made popular by influencers in North America, the United Kingdom or Australia travel through short video, podcasts and reposted clips, and they are read through local histories, economies and existing struggles over gender and sexuality. Feminist scholar Cynthia Enloe argues that the personal is international: everyday relationships are shaped by governments, economies and global systems.

Governments get involved. In South Korea’s 2022 presidential election, 58.7% of men in their twenties voted for the winner, who campaigned on an anti-feminist platform, and 58% of women in their twenties voted for his opponent. South Korea has one of the widest gender pay gaps in the OECD and the lowest birth rate in the world, and a small feminist movement called 4B urges women to give up dating, marriage and having children.

Platforms carry the same content into different conditions. The Guardian reported in April 2026 on a growing manosphere across Africa, with influencers earning money through platform creator programs. A researcher interviewed for a BBC World Service investigation said the content reaches young men with mental-health struggles in economies with high unemployment. In Latin America, one analysis argues the manosphere did not invent machismo but gave it a bigger audience. So these ideas are not simply imported from the Global North. They grow where gender inequality already exists and spread through platforms owned by Northern companies.

We know less than we should. Most of the surveys cited above come from the United States and the United Kingdom, and Equimundo is only now extending its State of the World’s Men study to countries including Brazil, South Africa, Argentina, Mexico and Pakistan. For much of the Global South, the evidence is journalism and a handful of investigations rather than large surveys. That matters for justice, because policy follows the data that exists.

Intersectionality, a concept from Kimberlé Crenshaw, explains why a universal story about “men” and “women” is incomplete. Gender is lived alongside race, class, disability, sexuality and nationality. A Black woman, a migrant man, a queer teenager and a wealthy heterosexual influencer meet online gender politics from different positions, with unequal power to shape the conversation. The 2025 review above notes that some influencers borrow the language of racial justice or religion to build trust while promoting restrictive ideas about gender, so it helps to judge the message, not the messenger’s identity.

A global conversation about equality must make room for different experiences without turning any group into a stereotype.

UN Women and UNODC estimated that about 50,000 women and girls were killed by intimate partners or other family members in 2024, roughly 137 every day, and Africa had the highest rate of any region. These deaths cannot be traced to any one online movement or ideology. They are a reminder that gender inequality and violence are real-world problems, not only internet debates. The UN Secretary-General’s report on violence against women also found that the growth of the manosphere coincides with rising conservatism among young men, who see efforts toward women’s equality as discrimination against men.

Global estimates depend on available national data, and reporting gaps remain. This statistic describes killings by partners or family members. It is not a measure of online misogyny.

08 / Beyond the feed

What does a better conversation look like?

Rejecting misogyny does not require rejecting men. Supporting women does not require treating every man as an enemy. A more equal digital culture needs room for honest talk about gendered pressures, alongside accountability for content that dehumanizes or stereotypes people.

  1. Separate a real problem from the scapegoat. Loneliness, economic stress and relationship difficulties deserve serious answers. Blaming an entire gender is not one.
  2. Check the evidence before sharing. Look for the original source, the research method, who was studied, and what the findings can actually establish.
  3. Notice the business model. Ask what the creator gains when you feel angry, afraid, inadequate or dependent on their advice.
  4. Make room for complexity. Men can experience gendered pressures while women face structural inequality. Recognizing one does not cancel the other.
  5. Build alternatives. Support conversations about healthy relationships, emotional literacy, consent, equality, and models of masculinity that do not depend on dominance. Equimundo and Futures Without Violence suggest focusing on young men “in the middle,” who are not committed to these ideas and may be open to a conversation.
The point is not to choose a pill. It is to refuse the idea that social progress requires one gender to lose for the other to win.
09 / About this project

Why I made it this way

This blog is my knowledge mobilization project. Its audience is young adults who use social media and dating apps, since that is where they meet these ideas. Its purpose is to explain two online movements in plain language, show who profits and who is harmed, and connect them to global gender justice. I chose a blog because it can hold both a readable argument and a full list of sources, and each section can be shared on its own. I avoided blaming men as a group, because most of the men drawn to this content are lonely rather than violent, and the aim is to reach people who are still open to other explanations.
